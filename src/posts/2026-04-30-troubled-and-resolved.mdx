> Now is my soul troubled; and what shall I say? Father, save me from this hour: but for this cause came I unto this hour. Father, glorify thy name. — John 12:27-28 (KJV)

"Now my soul is troubled." Not a metaphor. Not a rhetorical move. Jesus felt the weight of what was coming and said so out loud. He was three days from the cross. He knew it. And the knowing troubled Him.

## Both Things at Once

What He did not do: push through the emotion and pretend it wasn't there. He didn't perform calm for the crowd or find a way to reframe it. He said His soul was troubled. Directly, in public, without embarrassment. Feeling the weight of obedience is not a sign something is wrong with your faith.

And then, in the same breath, resolved: "This is why I came. Father, glorify thy name."

Not the emotion fading first. Not a pep talk to get the feeling under control. Both things at once, troubled and resolved, in the same sentence, with a voice from Heaven coming down in response.

## What Real Obedience Looks Like

The Greek word translated "troubled" here is tarasso. It's the same word used when the disciples thought they saw a ghost walking on the water. Not mild discomfort. Genuine distress.

Jesus was genuinely distressed. And genuinely obedient. At the same time.

This is what real faith looks like, not the stained-glass version where everything feels peaceful once you decide to follow God. Real faith is obedience that moves forward through the emotion, not around it. The trouble doesn't mean you got it wrong. It might mean you're in exactly the right place.

What is God asking you to do right now that troubles you? Not because you doubt Him, but because you feel the actual weight of it. Teaching. Having an honest conversation. Standing for something when standing will cost you.

Stay troubled and resolved. That's not a contradiction. That's the example.
